This issue's recommendations focus on the two agent harness projects most worth watching in the August 2026 AI open-source landscape: DeepSeek Harness (dsh for short), officially open-sourced by DeepSeek AI, and Apache Maka (Incubating), newly accepted into the Apache Software Foundation Incubator. The two are not copies of each other; rather, they answer the same question of the era with sharply different engineering philosophies — as large-model capabilities converge toward homogeneity, who gets to define how an agent actually "gets work done." DeepSeek Harness flies the banner of "Everything is a Plugin," turning the model, tools, sessions, sandboxes, and even the agent loop itself into pluggable components, and it accumulated over 210k stars on GitHub in under a month, becoming one of the fastest-growing repositories in history. Apache Maka, meanwhile, holds high the twin banners of "local-first" and "The Log is the Runtime," recording every model message, tool call, permission decision, and termination event as an append-only RuntimeEvent log, and — under the same model and the same official verifier — it published a nine-harness head-to-head benchmark in which its 77.5% pass rate edged out DeepSeek Harness's 73.0% for second place. These two projects form a combination full of tension: one represents the vendor-driven, community-exploding open-ecosystem path, the other the foundation-governed, data-driven neutral path. Putting them side by side is itself the best specimen for understanding the competition in agent infrastructure.

## 1. Background: why "Harness" became the keyword of 2026

### 1.1 The paradigm shift from "the model race" to "the framework race"

In the AI technology narrative of 2026, a clear paradigm shift has already taken place: the center of industry discussion is moving from "which model is stronger" to "what kind of harness the model runs inside." Agent harness, by the commonly accepted industry definition, refers to all the code, configuration, and execution logic outside the model — the loop that keeps calling the model, the tool inventory, the sandbox environment, the memory mechanism, and the rules that decide what enters the context window. In a cross-comparison survey published in August 2026, the Winder.AI team broke the AI technology stack into four layers — model, harness, framework, and platform — and made clear that the harness, as the middle layer that "runs a single agent," matters more in terms of which stack layer it fits than in its feature list. This judgment is backed by solid empirical evidence: Vercel raised the same model's success rate from 80% to 100% by removing 80% of the agent's tools; LangChain lifted a coding agent's score on Terminal Bench from 52.8% to 66.5% merely by switching harnesses; and Princeton's CORE-Bench tests show the same model scoring anywhere from 42% to 78% under different scaffolds. It is fair to say that, with model fundamentals converging, the harness has been promoted from a "wrapper shell" to the key infrastructure that decides an agent's success or failure.

### 1.2 The "same-day coincidence" and historical positioning of the two projects

There is a newsworthy coincidence between the two projects recommended in this issue: on August 13, 2026, DeepSeek AI announced an API price increase for its V4 Pro model at the same time as it open-sourced its Harness, and the Apache Software Foundation formally accepted Maka into the Incubator on that very same day. This date carries symbolic weight in the history of AI open source — vendor-driven open source and foundation-governed open source each placed a major piece on the board on the same day. In terms of product form, DeepSeek Harness is a pure runtime framework, officially positioned as the latter term in the formula "Agent = Model + Harness," deeply bound to the DeepSeek V4 model family, and interpreted by multiple media outlets as an open, buildable alternative to Anthropic's Claude Code. Apache Maka, by contrast, is a complete desktop workbench product, described in English as a "high-performance agent workspace that keeps a complete record of everything it did." Its repository was created on May 27, 2026, and it already had roughly ten weeks of intensive development history when it entered incubation. One leans "framework," the other leans "product"; one emphasizes replaceability, the other recordability — together they cover the two ends of the agent harness spectrum, which is precisely the core reason for featuring them in the same issue.

## 2. DeepSeek Harness: the open-source agent runtime where everything is a plugin

### 2.1 Project overview and explosive popularity

DeepSeek Harness is officially developed by DeepSeek AI, open-sourced under the MIT license, with dsh as its command-line entry point. The official project homepage summarizes its core proposition in a single sentence — everything is a plugin: model, tools, skills, sessions, sandbox, storage, loop, scheduling, and user interface — every capability can be replaced or recombined. The official documentation notes that DeepSeek's own model benchmarks use the Harness's minimal mode as their framework, and that Code Agent text-task tests for the DeepSeek model family all use the combination of the max tier and temperature=1.0. The project's popularity trajectory is extremely rare in open-source history: a hands-on article on cnblogs recorded it hitting 50k stars within 12 hours of open-sourcing, with 288 third-party plugin repositories tagged dsh-plugin appearing on release day itself; a Medium report put it at 141,000 stars in four days; and Winder.AI's survey recorded 95,386 stars in two days. It should be noted that figures sampled by different outlets at different times vary considerably, and Wavect specifically cautions that the claim of "fastest growing in GitHub history" has not been officially certified by GitHub — when citing, use real-time data from the official repository and note the sampling time.

### 2.2 Core architecture: the Cordis kernel and the plugin-based runtime

The key to understanding DeepSeek Harness lies in its underlying Cordis meta-framework. The official preview page describes Cordis as the "kernel" responsible for managing plugin mounting, unmounting, and dependency relationships; every capability of the agent exists as a plugin, and Cordis's service and event mechanisms let plugins work in concert. Developers can select, replace, or extend any capability through configuration alone, without modifying the framework's source code. A Chinese-language analysis by Eigent AI further clarifies the boundaries of pluginization: not only are tools and skills plugins, but so are the model adapters themselves, sessions and runtime state, the sandbox environment, storage locations, loop and scheduling logic, and even the UI itself. The direct corollary of this architecture is that "there is no privileged kernel that needs patching" — the official documentation states explicitly that the way to extend dsh is to mount plugins alongside other plugins, rather than building peripheral extensions around a closed core. A DataCamp comparison article corroborates this design from an engineering perspective: Cordis lets plugins discover services and exchange events, dependency changes load or unload plugins, hot reloads clean up stale listeners and background jobs, and the default profile exposes the loop as configuration. The contrast with Claude Code highlights the difference most sharply: the latter offers peripheral extension mechanisms such as skills, hooks, and MCP around a fixed built-in loop, whereas Harness plugins can directly define components of the runtime itself — that is, "DeepSeek Harness can host Claude, but Claude Code cannot host DeepSeek."

### 2.3 Four run modes and the agent preset system

DeepSeek Harness ships with four officially designed run modes, which are essentially different combination presets drawn from the same plugin library. Standard mode is the full-featured coding agent, covering the complete set of capabilities: file editing, Shell, file and web retrieval, Skills, plan mode, goal tracking, sub-agents, and workflows. Code Mode (PTC mode) additionally provides the Code Mode SDK on top of standard mode, allowing the model to write TypeScript programs that compose multi-step operations instead of issuing multiple independent tool calls — well suited to batch-operation scenarios. Minimal mode keeps only two tools, a persistent bash and str_replace_editor, serving as the clean environment the official team uses for benchmarking and minimal reproduction; the official documentation confirms that all public benchmarks of the DeepSeek model family use this mode. Creator mode targets the advanced scenario of "using an agent to build agents": on top of everything in standard mode it adds runtime inspection, plugin experimentation, and preset authoring guidance, letting users inspect the current runtime, test Cordis plugins in memory, and compose them into new modes. The progression among the four modes is clear: standard mode does the work, code mode orchestrates, minimal mode evaluates, creator mode evolves — this systematized preset design is itself a product highlight worth unpacking.

### 2.4 Observability design: Trajectory view and append-only session logs

DeepSeek Harness's investment in observability reflects how seriously it takes "traceability." The official preview page explicitly promises that "every run is traceable": everything the model receives is recorded in an append-only session log, including the system prompt, reasoning process, tool calls and results, sub-agent scheduling, and all context injections. The Trajectory view lets users browse these records by source, and resume, fork, search, and replay operations are all built on the same event stream. A QbitAI hands-on article, republished with authorization by 36Kr, describes this feature's practical value in detail: the longer an agent runs and the more complex its toolchain becomes, the more the chat summary turns into a black box, while the trajectory feature provides a replay window for monitoring the agent at any time — you can see the raw event-level records directly and pinpoint exactly which step tripped the model up and how much money each stage burned. DataCamp's assessment is "model-visible means logged," noting that the run-path detail it presents in the UI is richer than Claude Code's JSONL records. Notably, this append-only log design belongs to the same frontier direction as Apache Maka's RuntimeEvent log: a comparison article on aiguide.cc lists the two side by side as representatives of the "the record is the truth" route — starting from different origins, the two products converge on the principle of "the log as the single source of truth."

### 2.5 Plugin ecosystem and community organization

DeepSeek Harness's ecosystem-building strategy shows a distinctly "operating-system-like" character. The QbitAI hands-on article likens it to "the Android of the agent era": the official distribution already includes more than a hundred built-in plugins, the community can install third-party plugins the way Minecraft players install mods, and the repository documentation even contains a complete step-by-step tutorial for building a plugin from scratch and integrating it with the official Harness, plus a built-in Skill for modding Cordis. The official preview page provides an entry point to the community plugin index (the dsh-plugin topic tag on GitHub), welcomes Harness developers to join the DSH plugin ecosystem, and hosts community collaboration through GitHub Discussions and Discord. The cnblogs hands-on test further reveals a key capability: Harness ships with Providers that invoke Claude Code and Codex as sub-agents, implementing delegated execution by resolving the corresponding binaries on the PATH — which means users can use Harness as the upper-layer orchestrator, folding competing tools into their own workflows rather than replacing them. Winder.AI's survey confirms this too, pointing out that Zed Agent, OpenHands, and DeepSeek Harness all support the Agent Client Protocol and can drive one another — interoperability between harnesses is becoming an industry trend.

### 2.6 Getting started and developer experience

The onboarding bar for DeepSeek Harness can be summarized as "five minutes to launch, learning required to go deep." The fastest official way to start is to run `npx @deepseek-ai/dsh web` in an environment with Node.js installed; it starts a Web UI at http://127.0.0.1:3080 by default and opens the browser automatically, and when launched over SSH it only prints the host address for local forwarding. Running from source requires cloning the repository and then executing `pnpm install`, `pnpm run build`, and `pnpm dsh web` in sequence. First-time use requires users to configure their own model API key — both the official preview page and the 36Kr hands-on article confirm that Harness does not ship with a shared model account, and users can plug into the DeepSeek open platform or models from other providers. The cnblogs hands-on test notes that the interface is currently dominated by the web version, which is not friendly enough for terminal-habituated developers, but that the Headless mode and official Python SDK support programmatic batch execution — running unattended tasks file by file or module by module, a capability that significantly outperforms interactive tools in scenarios like bulk code migration. DataCamp's comparison table records its interfaces as a local Web UI, a headless CLI, and a Python SDK. The two reviews describe CLI status with a time-basis discrepancy (the cnblogs article was published on August 20, 2026, the DataCamp article on August 24), so the prudent phrasing is "early versions center on the Web UI, with the headless CLI and Python SDK being added progressively during iteration" — and readers should be reminded that this product iterates extremely fast and its feature matrix may change at any time.

## 3. Apache Maka: the local-first agent workbench that records everything

### 3.1 Project overview and the road to Apache incubation

Apache Maka is the first project accepted by the Apache Software Foundation Incubator with an agent harness as its core positioning. The official incubator page describes it as "a local-first AI agent runtime and workspace that records model messages, tool calls, tool results, permission decisions, and termination events in an append-only log." The project is hosted by the Apache Incubator PMC, with tison (Zili Chen) as Champion, four Mentors, and a committer list of seven people including Kun Li, Jie Wen, and Yuhan Lei. The repository was created on May 27, 2026; on July 14, when the project team posted a sharing thread in the v2ex community, it had only 666 stars and 70 forks. After entering the Incubator on August 13, attention climbed rapidly — aiguide.cc recorded about 2,381 stars around August 24, agentconn.com recorded 3.6k stars on August 26, and at the time of this writing (September 9, 2026) the official repository shows 5.1k stars, 476 forks, and 4507 commits. Although this growth curve falls far short of DeepSeek Harness's explosive trajectory, together with topping GitHub Trending on August 23, 2026 and taking the day's repository star in the TypeScript category on August 24, it is more than enough to support the recommendation positioning of "one of the most-watched new AI projects in the Apache ecosystem."

### 3.2 Core philosophy: The Log is the Runtime

Maka's product philosophy is condensed into a formula that recurs across its website: State(t) = Project(Log[0…t]) — the system's state at any moment is the result of projecting the ordered log. The official README concretizes this as the "The Log is the Runtime" principle: every model message, tool call, permission decision, and termination event is an append-only RuntimeEvent; the interface, the next prompt, and crash recovery are all projections of this log, never the sole copy; old tool output can leave the next prompt without ever leaving the log. A technical analysis on CSDN explains the intellectual source of this design from a distributed-systems perspective: it is the log-first idea brought to the agent domain — first persist ordered, unambiguous facts of change, then let consumers rebuild their own state, thereby reliably answering key questions such as "what had the model seen before a given event boundary, what tools had it called, which permissions had been requested or decided." The practical value of this design is expressed more plainly in the project team's v2ex sharing thread: there are already plenty of AI desktops on the market, and what Maka wants to solve is not "whether the chat interface looks good" but "whether the process of agent execution should be preserved as a first-class citizen." The project slogan is "Maka preserves experience as evidence." "The Log is the Runtime" is a narrative anchor that is both technically deep and easy to explain — it unifies the three abstract selling points of "auditable, recoverable, reproducible" under a single visualizable principle.

### 3.3 Unified Runtime Host and the three-entry architecture

Another highlight of Maka's architecture worth recommending is the "One Runtime Host" principle. The official README makes it explicit: Desktop, TUI and CLI, and Eval are all thin clients; execution is decided solely by this one Runtime Host, and Eval owns only experiments and scores. This means the desktop GUI, the terminal TUI/CLI, and the evaluation framework share exactly the same execution path, so evaluation results migrate seamlessly into production scenarios. This design eliminates the classic engineering trap of "evaluation environment diverging from production environment." aiguide.cc's review lists this as an innovation: desktop, CLI, and Eval share one runtime; evaluation results are reproducible; experiments and production use the same execution path. At the entry-point level, the desktop side is a local application built on Electron plus React, offering streaming sessions, a tool timeline, branching and resume, and other features; the terminal entry supports launching the TUI with `npm run cli` or executing a single Turn; and the Eval entry supports declarative multi-arm experiments (task × repetition × subject), outputting a results kernel of scores, usage, cost, and duration. In addition, Maka ships with six basic tools — Read, Write, Edit, Bash, Glob, Grep — and provides provider-agnostic Model Context Protocol client integration, so MCP tools automatically inherit the existing permission controls, runtime event logging, and telemetry. This complete "workbench + evaluation framework" product form gives Maka a differentiated position between "pure framework" and "pure chat tool."

### 3.4 Letting the data speak: the Terminal-Bench 2.1 nine-harness shootout

Maka's sharpest material is its published cross-evaluation data. The core tagline on the project homepage is "Measured, not claimed," and two sets of test results are publicly displayed. The first is a nine-harness comparison running all 89 Terminal-Bench 2.1 tasks on DeepSeek V4 Flash, uniformly scored by the official verifier: Codex ranks first with an 82.0% pass rate, Maka second at 77.5%, Pi at 74.2%, DeepSeek Harness (minimal mode) at 73.0%, ZCode at 70.8%, Reasonix at 67.4%, OpenCode at 65.2%, Kimi Code at 59.6%, and Claude Code last at 55.1%. The second is a paired single-run comparison between Maka and OpenCode, with a McNemar test confirming the gap is significant (p = 0.0118): Maka leads 68.5% to 55.1% by 13.5 percentage points, at essentially identical cost per passed task. A Zhihu article's reading of this data is highly shareable: same model, same reasoning max tier, and first place is separated from last by 26.9 percentage points — a full 24 tasks; of the 89 tasks, only 28 were passed by all nine harnesses and 5 were failed by all of them, while the remaining 56 (about 63%) produced different results on different harnesses. The article further notes that the harness is becoming the most important — and most easily overlooked — layer of infrastructure beyond model capability, and that Maka chose to enter Apache precisely to hand this layer over to an open, neutral, long-lived community governance. One thing must be honestly flagged: this shootout was led by the Maka project team itself, and although the test report, per-task results, and experimental methodology have all been published with a reproduction PR attached, no independent third party has yet fully reproduced it, so citations should note the attribute of "data published by the project team."

### 3.5 Security, permissions, and local-first privacy design

Maka's third core selling point is a security design built around local data sovereignty. The official README promises "your data stays on your machine, you bring the model": sessions, settings, and run records are stored on the local machine, and users bring their own model — cloud APIs, local models, or compatible gateways all work. Workspace data lives by default under the Electron userData directory, including runtime.sqlite, the connections directory, the credential vault, and settings files; secrets such as API keys are stored in a local file, credential-vault.json, readable only by the system account, so the renderer process (the UI process) never sees plaintext. At the tool-execution layer, tools that write files or run Shell must first cross the sandbox boundary, out-of-bounds operations must request permission approval, runs can be aborted, and failures are categorized. CSDN's architecture analysis adds finer-grained security measures: stdio inherits only a whitelist of environment variables needed to run by default; all MCP tools are treated as mutations requiring explicit authorization; the main process performs runtime validation of IPC payloads; configuration files use strict 0600/0700 file permissions; and aggregate boundary checks are applied to model text, image count, and total size. agentconn.com's review points out in particular that Maka's append-only log natively supports crash recovery and resuming interrupted tasks — highly attractive to users who "have lost work to an agent session crash" — while its local-first architecture also means there is no built-in cross-device sync. Together these features make Maka the recommended first choice in privacy-sensitive scenarios (such as sensitive enterprise codebases and compliance audit needs), creating a clear scenario split with DeepSeek Harness's cloud-model route.

### 3.6 The density and speed of community collaboration

If DeepSeek Harness's community advantage lies in scale, Maka's lies in density. The Zhihu article, citing project-team statistics, reports that in roughly ten weeks after the repository was created on May 27, Maka accumulated about 710k lines of TypeScript (of which about 350k lines are tests), 949 test files, 2,439 commits, 1,218 merged PRs, a 93.8% PR merge rate, a median PR merge time of about 33.5 minutes, and 943 PRs merged in July alone. The v2ex sharing thread adds another set of early data: 300 commits in 9 days (111 fix, 103 feat), averaging 33 commits per day, with a core of two or three contributors. Especially noteworthy is the community's self-organizing capacity: two days before release, a community user raised the need to align with full provider support on models.dev and opened it for claiming; within two days the community successively contributed more than 20 provider integrations, covering Cloudflare Workers AI, Hugging Face, Vercel AI Gateway, Cohere, Mistral, xAI, Together AI, and domestic services such as Volcano Ark, Tencent, and StepFun. This builder-community temperament of high-frequency collaboration and rapid validation is a rather rare "new species" in the Apache Incubator. Of course, the other side must also be presented honestly: in the v2ex thread the project team admitted that "the sophistication of the runtime design has outpaced the product loop and the newcomer onboarding experience — developers encountering it for the first time struggle to get started within five minutes and clearly view a complete Agent Run," which is a priority direction for subsequent optimization.

## 4. Head-to-head comparison: how the two products perform on the same benchmark and in the same coordinate system

### 4.1 Differences in positioning and design philosophy

The most essential difference between the two products lies in their answers to "what a harness should be." DeepSeek Harness's philosophy is "replaceability first": the official preview page lists "everything is a plugin" as the first design principle, emphasizing that developers can select, replace, or extend any capability through configuration. Its metaphor is an operating system or a LEGO baseplate — Eigent AI positions it as "the runtime layer that wraps the language model," while QbitAI calls it outright "the Android of the agent era." Maka's philosophy is "recordability first": all four core features in the official README revolve around measurement and recording — measured, not claimed; the log is the runtime; your data on your machine; one Runtime Host. Its metaphor is a ledger or a black box — committing every "behavioral transaction" of the agent to disk in a tamper-proof way. aiguide.cc's comparison table captures this divide precisely in one line: Maka is a "local-first agent workspace (the record is the truth)," while DeepSeek Harness is an "everything-is-a-plugin agent runtime framework." Once you understand this philosophical difference, the two products' differences on other dimensions acquire a unified explanatory frame: DSH sacrificed stability commitments for replaceability (developer preview, breaking-change warnings), while Maka sacrificed ecosystem scale for recordability (incubation period, no formal release).

### 4.2 Direct confrontation on performance and cost under the same benchmark

The direct comparison data for the two products on the same benchmark is the most persuasive material. In the Terminal-Bench 2.1 nine-harness shootout, Maka's 77.5% pass rate leads DeepSeek Harness (minimal mode) at 73.0% by about 4.5 percentage points. On the cost dimension the gap between the two is almost negligible: Maka costs about $0.0263 per passed task and DSH about $0.0262, while last-place Claude Code runs as high as $0.0537 — more than double either of them; the cheapest of the whole group is Pi at $0.0197. Based on this, the Zhihu article drew a two-dimensional "pass rate vs. cost" chart, noting that Codex, Maka, and Pi form the Pareto frontier, and that Maka achieved the second-place pass rate at a cost close to DSH's — a textbook case of "striking a balance between performance and economy." A third-party 30-task comparison cited in the cnblogs hands-on article corroborates the cost advantage of the harness route from another angle: Pi Agent costs about $0.028 per successful task versus Claude Code at $0.195 — a difference of nearly 7x. What must be honestly flagged to readers: in this comparison DSH ran in minimal mode rather than its standard mode, and the test was led by the Maka project team, which published the report and per-task CSV but whose independent reproduction is still pending. Moreover, whether the 4.5-percentage-point difference between DSH and Maka is stable is subject to model sampling randomness in a single run — but the structural phenomenon that 56 of 89 tasks (63%) changed outcomes with the harness is enough to support the core thesis that "choosing a harness is itself a performance decision."

### 4.3 Contrasts in licensing, governance, and ecosystem structure

On the licensing and governance dimension, the two products have chosen sharply different but internally coherent paths. DeepSeek Harness uses the MIT license, one of the most permissive open-source licenses, allowing anyone to use, modify, and build commercial products on top of the code. Its governance is led by the single vendor DeepSeek, with community participation through GitHub Discussions, Discord, and the dsh-plugin topic tag, and the official side makes clear that "core plugins and APIs will keep evolving." Maka uses Apache License 2.0 and receives neutral governance from the Apache Software Foundation: decisions are made in public, the community governs together, and intellectual property is vetted through the incubator process. The incubator page shows the project has been in incubation for only about a month and still has substantial procedural work ahead — copyright confirmation, distribution-rights verification, committer agreements — and incubation status "indicates that the project has not yet been fully endorsed by the ASF." In ecosystem structure, DSH has already formed the embryo of a plugin marketplace — 288 dsh-plugin repositories on release day and 100+ officially built-in plugins; Maka's ecosystem centers on provider integrations via models.dev and community contributions, with 20+ provider supports in two days as its signature achievement. This is a classic "vendor speed vs. foundation credit" contrast: the former is fast but may change, the latter slow but predictable. The "long-term credibility" that Apache incubation branding brings Maka was listed by aiguide.cc as its top advantage, while Winder.AI's assessment of DSH — "iterating extremely fast; all features are currently unstable" — reminds us of the true state of its developer-preview period.

### 4.4 Maturity, version status, and risk exposure

Both products are early in maturity, but the nature of their risks differs. DeepSeek Harness is explicitly in developer preview; its official README warns that "compatibility-breaking changes are possible" and advises reviewing security advisories before running. Wavect's enterprise-grade evaluation concludes it is "well suited for scoped engineering pilots, but not yet a production control plane you can adopt on hype alone." Maka likewise "has not published an official Apache release": the official side offers only two paths — the Desktop Nightly built daily from the main branch (Windows and Linux builds are unsigned previews) and building from source — and the README makes clear that Nightly "is not an ASF release and is not suitable for production use." On versions, the SourceForge mirror directory shows a development build v0.2.0-dev.15.20260902, while v0.1.11 on GitHub Releases (August 18, 2026) did not go through the ASF release process. An honest risk warning is indispensable: neither product should be recommended to production users who "need stability"; their recommendation value lies in technology exploration, learning, and pilot scenarios.

### 4.5 Platform support, barriers to entry, and onboarding paths

On platform support and onboarding barriers, each product has its strengths and weaknesses. DeepSeek Harness is a pure Node.js project: a single npx command launches the Web UI on any platform with Node.js installed, with no platform binding; its sandbox uses bubblewrap or Landlock on Linux, Seatbelt on macOS, and ACL-restricted tokens on Windows, with permission presets including read-only, workspace-write, and danger-full-access. Maka's desktop side is Electron-based, with official Nightlies covering macOS (Apple Silicon and Intel), Windows x64, and Linux x64 and arm64, though the Windows and Linux builds are unsigned previews; some third-party reviews (aiguide.cc, agentconn.com) recorded a more conservative platform reality — macOS Apple Silicon as the main supported platform, with Linux not yet officially supported — which differs from the official README due to a time gap, so the prudent phrasing is "official Nightlies already cover the three major platforms, but Windows/Linux are unsigned previews, and third-party reviews recorded more limited availability at earlier points in time." On onboarding barriers: DSH starts with a single command, but deep use requires understanding the Cordis plugin mechanism, Profiles, and bundle concepts; Maka's first run requires configuring a model connection yourself (enter settings to add an API, a local model, or an already-connected account connection and test it), and the build environment requires Node.js 22.19 or above, npm, Git, and ripgrep — developing Peer mode additionally requires Rust 1.98 or above. The project team itself acknowledges that Maka's "newcomer onboarding experience" is its weak point, while DSH's "configuration learning cost" is likewise a complaint repeatedly raised by reviewers — the two products unexpectedly agree on this "common ailment of early-stage tools."

## 5. In-depth analysis of strengths and weaknesses: from evaluation evidence to recommendation conclusions

### 5.1 DeepSeek Harness's strengths

DeepSeek Harness's first strength is **an architectural openness unmatched among its peers**. The official side defines the model, tools, skills, sessions, sandbox, storage, loop, scheduling, and even the UI as replaceable plugins; this thoroughness means teams can assemble a fully customized agent runtime rather than patching around an existing product. The ability demonstrated in the cnblogs hands-on test — "invoking Claude Code and Codex as sub-agents" — proves this architecture's real power: users can keep their investment in existing tools and use Harness as the upper-layer orchestrator. The second strength is **cost structure**: under the same model, DSH costs about $0.0262 per passed task, less than half of Claude Code, and combined with the roughly 99% cache hit rate (occasionally fluctuating to 60-70%) recorded in the 36Kr hands-on article and the real-time token-consumption dashboard, this constitutes a real attraction for price-sensitive developers. The third strength is **observability**: the event-level replay provided by the Trajectory view, the ability to browse records by source, and the "model-visible means logged" principle turn agent behavior from a black box into an auditable object. The fourth strength is **ecosystem momentum**: 217k stars of attention, 288 third-party plugin repositories on release day, and 100+ official built-in plugins mean the problems a developer encounters have very likely already been hit by community forerunners. The fifth strength is **headless batch capability**: the official Python SDK supports programmatic loops that execute tasks module by module — irreplaceable by interactive tools in scenarios such as cross-repository code migration, batch testing, and unattended pipelines.

### 5.2 DeepSeek Harness's weaknesses and risks

Its shortcomings are as clear as its strengths. First is **maturity risk**: developer-preview status combined with officially flagged potential breaking changes means any production system built on the current version must budget for migration costs; Wavect advises "pinning a version and reserving migration budget," and warns "do not ship customer-facing autonomous operations on preview defaults." Second is **doubt over the completeness of the security boundary**: Wavect's evaluation explicitly notes that its filesystem sandbox does not cover network access or process visibility, and that older Landlock environments and the current Windows ACL boundary may provide only partial enforcement; enterprise deployments need to add their own network policies, separate identities, plugin review, and adversarial testing. Third is **measured reliability issues**: in AtlasCloud's evaluation, three identical task runs all claimed completion yet only one page was actually usable; the same evaluation also found that two lines of YAML configuration undocumented on the docs site (compat.thinkingFormat and maxTokens) could shorten a task from 36 steps and 422 seconds to 15 steps and 152 seconds — indirect evidence that documentation lags implementation. Fourth is **resource-footprint controversy**: AtlasCloud recorded npm installation downloading 531 packages totaling 306 MB (the package itself is only 172 KB); Hacker News users reported 1.5 GB after build and 500 MB of idle-session memory; although the same evaluation noted the web server occupies only 35-40 MB RSS at idle, community skepticism about the dependency chain is real. Fifth is **the configuration learning curve**: the Cordis plugin mechanism, Profiles, and bundle concepts pose a barrier for users who just want "out of the box" — both the cnblogs and DataCamp reviews mention the steps of configuring providers and getting familiar with profiles.

### 5.3 Apache Maka's strengths

Maka's first strength is **governance credit**: Apache Software Foundation incubation brings a long-term governance commitment — the project belongs to no model vendor, decisions are made in public, intellectual property is vetted by the incubator, and the license is Apache 2.0. aiguide.cc lists "high long-term credibility" as its top advantage, and agentconn.com comments that "the Apache Foundation's backing provides confidence in its long-term maintenance." The second strength is **a culture of published evaluation**: the project writes "measured, not claimed" into the first line of its official README, attaches a per-task CSV to every evaluation report it publishes, and disclosed the statistical-test details of its paired comparison with OpenCode (McNemar test, p = 0.0118). This practice of handing the power of evaluation to reproducible data rather than marketing language is genuinely rare in the agent-tool ecosystem and gives media outlets a rare "verifiable" source. The third strength is **audit-grade execution records**: the append-only RuntimeEvent log covers messages, tool calls, permission decisions, and termination events, and together with the context-trimming strategy of "old tool output can leave the next prompt without leaving the log," it gives agent sessions the triple properties of reproducibility, recoverability, and auditability — and "shorter context does not mean deleted history" resolves the tension between performance and evidence retention. The fourth strength is **local data sovereignty**: sessions, keys, and run records stay on the local machine by default; the model can be cloud or local; and together with sandbox out-of-bounds approval, 0600/0700 file permissions, IPC payload validation, and other designs, this constitutes a complete solution for privacy-sensitive scenarios. The fifth strength is **product completeness**: the three entries — desktop, TUI/CLI, and Eval — share a single Runtime Host, and the design where evaluation is the production path avoids environment drift, which is a unique selling point when recommending it to technical teams that "need both daily use and evaluation."

### 5.4 Apache Maka's weaknesses and risks

Maka's weaknesses likewise need candid presentation. First is **the release-status gap**: the project has no official Apache release; the official README does not recommend prebuilt downloads, so users must either use the unstable Nightly preview or build from source — a substantive barrier for ordinary users. Second is **platform coverage still ramping**: the official Nightly's Windows and Linux builds are both unsigned previews, and third-party reviews at earlier points even recorded "Linux not yet supported," leaving significant uncertainty about the actual experience for non-macOS users. Third is **limited ecosystem size**: at 5.1k stars and 476 forks (as read), it is far smaller than competitors such as DeepSeek Harness, Codex, and OpenCode, and plugins and third-party integrations are still accumulating; aiguide.cc explicitly notes that "the project is young, and its ecosystem, plugins, and third-party integrations are still building up." Fourth is **the relatively high onboarding bar**: the project team itself admits that "the sophistication of the runtime design has outpaced the product loop and newcomer onboarding experience," and the build environment requires Node.js 22.19 or above, ripgrep, and even the Rust toolchain (Peer mode) — not friendly to beginners. Fifth is **data-format stability risk**: the official README states clearly that "Maka is still in active development, and data formats, CLI commands, and experiment capabilities may still change," and that after upgrades old JSONL records are not imported and credentials must be re-entered — meaning migration costs for early users with existing data. Taken together, Maka's positioning should focus on the triangle of "advanced technical philosophy, solid governance credit, productization still early," avoiding over-promising.

## 6. Writing guide and material distillation

### 6.1 Narrative arc and structural suggestions

This issue's recommendation can adopt the narrative arc of "one question, two routes, one shared arena." Open with the "same-day coincidence" of August 13, 2026 to create suspense: on the same day, a vendor open-sourced the fastest-growing agent framework in history, and a foundation accepted its first agent harness project. Then pose the question that runs through the piece: when the harness becomes a more important variable than the model, should developers choose "the replaceable open foundation" or "the recordable local workbench"? When introducing DeepSeek Harness, unfold from the concept of "everything is a plugin," use the four run modes as the skeleton, back the heat with 217k stars and 288 first-day plugin repositories, and argue practical value with the Trajectory view and cost data. When introducing Maka, open with the "The Log is the Runtime" formula, create comparative tension with the Terminal-Bench 2.1 figures of 77.5% versus 73.0%, and close with the Apache governance and local-first narrative. The comparison chapter can directly use the table from Section 4, paired with the single most shareable data point: "same model, different harness, and 63% of the tasks change outcomes." The ending should land on "choosing a philosophy" rather than "choosing a tool": pick DSH if you need self-building and orchestration, pick Maka if you need recording and auditing — and the two are not even mutually exclusive, since DSH's plugin architecture could in theory host a Maka-style logging strategy, while Maka's Eval framework can equally evaluate DSH.

### 6.2 Core data points and a library of quotable lines

The following data points and formulations, verified across multiple sources, can be safely cited with attribution. Popularity data: the official DeepSeek Harness repository has 217k stars, 25.7k forks, and 16089 commits (as read on September 9, 2026), and surpassed 121k stars within three calendar days of release; the Maka repository has 5.1k stars, 476 forks, and 4507 commits, and topped GitHub Trending on August 23, 2026. Performance data: the complete Terminal-Bench 2.1 nine-harness ranking from Codex at 82.0% down to Claude Code at 55.1%, with Maka second at 77.5% and DSH fourth at 73.0%; 56 of 89 tasks (about 63%) changed outcomes with the harness; on cost, Maka runs about $0.0263 per task, DSH about $0.0262 per task, and Claude Code $0.0537 per task. Quotable lines: "the Android of the agent era" (QbitAI's metaphor for DSH); "Measured, not claimed" and "State(t) = Project(Log[0…t])" (Maka's official taglines); "the model is the brain, the harness is the hands, feet, and nervous system" (cnblogs paraphrasing the official definition); "Claude Code is a move-in-ready furnished apartment; Harness is an open-floor-plan shell with prefab components, blueprints included." Community data: Maka's 710k lines of TypeScript, 350k lines of tests, 93.8% PR merge rate, and 33.5-minute median merge time in ten weeks; DSH's 288 dsh-plugin repositories on release day. When using these materials, write in the "as-of date" as well (e.g., "as of September 9"), because both products' star and version data are changing rapidly.

### 6.3 Phrasing pitfalls and fact-checking essentials

Pay special attention to the following phrasing pitfalls when writing. First, regarding DSH's interface form: cnblogs (August 20, 2026) says "currently web-only, no CLI," while DataCamp (August 24) records "local Web UI, headless CLI, and Python SDK" coexisting. Since the product iterates extremely fast, prefer the prudent phrasing "the Web UI is the main entry, with the headless CLI and Python SDK being added progressively during the preview period," and avoid absolute statements. Second, regarding Maka's platform support: there is a time gap between the official README's Nightly matrix (macOS AS/Intel, Windows x64, Linux x64/arm64) and some third-party reviews' more conservative records; follow the official account as primary and note that "third-party reviews were recorded at earlier points in time." Third, regarding star counts: DSH star figures sampled by different outlets at different times vary enormously (95k in two days, 141k in four days, 50k in 12 hours, etc.), so citations must note the sampling time or uniformly adopt the formulation "as read at the time of writing." Fourth, regarding ownership of the shootout: although the Terminal-Bench 2.1 nine-harness data is open and transparent, it was led by the Maka project team, so write it as "shootout data published by the Maka project team, with per-task CSV and a reproduction PR attached," avoiding giving readers the impression of "independent third-party evaluation." Fifth, regarding "fastest in history" formulations: Wavect explicitly cautions that this claim has not been certified by GitHub, so soften it to "an explosive speed rarely seen in open-source history." Sixth, regarding the v2ex hands-on data (60 million tokens, 97.5% cache hit rate, about 4 yuan in cost, scores of 0.8-0.9): this is self-reported by the project team and should be labeled "early hands-on data published by the project team" when cited.

### 6.4 Title directions and reader segmentation strategy

Based on the material characteristics of the two products, three types of title directions can be considered. Data-impact titles can use the shootout figures directly to create contrast, e.g., "Same DeepSeek V4 Flash, different harness — a 24-task gap: this issue recommends the two contenders separated by 4.5 percentage points." Concept-anchor titles can highlight each side's core proposition, e.g., "One turns everything into plugins, the other writes everything into a log: the two agent harnesses most worth following in 2026." Opinion-led titles suit deep readers, e.g., "The vendor's Android and the foundation's ledger: the battle of routes between DeepSeek Harness and Apache Maka." For reader segmentation, it is recommended to split the body into a "five-minute quick read" (one sentence of core positioning, one table of key data, one paragraph on target audience) and a "deep dive" (architecture principles, evaluation details, risk checklist), serving general tech readers and developer readers respectively. Suggested audience phrasing: DeepSeek Harness is recommended for developers and platform engineering teams who value open source and customization, need batch unattended tasks, want to orchestrate multiple AI tools into a unified workflow, are cost-sensitive, and can tolerate preview-period churn; Apache Maka is recommended for users who value execution auditability and data sovereignty, need to run agents locally, have compliance requirements or have lost work to session crashes, and are willing to accept the incubation period and the source-build bar. The end of the article should carry a unified "enter production environments with caution" notice plus links to each project's official repository and documentation — this reflects professionalism and matches both products' official self-positioning on version status.

## 7. Conclusion and outlook

The two projects recommended in this issue jointly corroborate an industry judgment now unfolding: in an era of converging model capabilities, the harness has become one of the decisive variables in agent performance, and "with what philosophy to build a harness" is a question more worth pondering than "which harness to use." With the MIT license, 217k stars, and the thoroughgoing openness of "everything is a plugin," DeepSeek Harness demonstrates the mobilization speed and expansion limits a vendor-driven open-source ecosystem can reach; with the Apache 2.0 license, neutral governance, and the recording philosophy of "The Log is the Runtime," Apache Maka proves the viability and differentiated value of the foundation route at the agent-infrastructure layer. Their direct confrontation on the same benchmark — Maka's 77.5% versus DSH's 73.0% pass rate, at nearly identical per-task cost — both shows the two are close in technical caliber as products of the same generation and hints that "competition between harnesses" will remain in dynamic contention for a long time.

Looking ahead, three directions are worth continuous tracking: first, when DeepSeek Harness ships its first stable release will directly determine whether it can move from "phenomenal preview" to "production-grade platform" — the official documentation already states that the current version is "not yet recommended for locking production systems to"; second, whether Apache Maka can complete its first official ASF release within the incubation period and improve its onboarding experience will test whether the "recording philosophy" has what it takes to productize — the project team has publicly acknowledged that the product loop is the weak point of the newcomer experience; third, whether the two routes the products represent — "pluginization" and "log-ification" — will converge: DSH's append-only session logs and Maka's RuntimeEvent have already been judged by third parties as "belonging to the same frontier direction," and the trend of harnesses driving one another through the Agent Client Protocol, even serving as each other's sub-agents, may ultimately turn "choosing one side" into "combining both sides." Quarterly revisits to the version pages and official announcements of these two repositories will be the lowest-cost strategy for extending this article's lifespan — because every upcoming release of these two projects may become a new topic in itself.
