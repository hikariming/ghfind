Let's get one thing out of the way first: ghfind is a site that scores developers based on public GitHub data — and the subject of this issue's pick has no GitHub account.

We decided to feature him anyway. Look at enough open-source projects and a pattern emerges: what decides how far a project goes is never just the code. We covered the code half last issue with Yuxuan Zhang of Z.ai. The other half — growth, distribution, community, monetization — needs people who are just as good at their craft. Max Liu (刘德泽) is a specimen of that other half: born in the 2000s, dropped out of the University of Auckland in 2023, cycled through six arenas in three years, grew an AI product's ARR from zero to $3 million, and finally turned down a chance to join Anthropic in favor of LobeHub, the open-source agent platform. For what it's worth, LobeHub's main repository sits in the ghfind graph with 30 scored contributors averaging 79.5 points and 80k+ stars — his taste in engineering teams holds up against the data.

## The dropout: a two-night decision, six weeks of persuasion

Max's family background, as he told 36Kr, is "two Tsinghua parents" — both graduated from Tsinghua University and now work as civil servants in Shandong. This family once did something very un-standard: when he was seven or eight, his mother pulled him out of school for a year to travel and learn around Mount Tai. It's hard to quantify what that year taught him, but it planted an idea: the standard path is something you're allowed to question.

In October 2023, during a summer trip home from Auckland where he was a third-year accounting and business student, Max watched the AI boom taking off in China and made the decision to drop out — in two nights. Convincing his parents took a month and a half: he built a full slide deck and pitched them his career plan "like presenting to a client." His core argument was a coldly reasoned supply-and-demand curve: get in now and the bar is low; wait until graduation and the industry may want a master's degree; wait for the master's and the bar may have moved to a PhD — and he wasn't an AI major to begin with. Every year of waiting raised the cost of entry exponentially.

That window analysis has held up since. His résumé lists his education as "college dropout," and it has never once cost him an offer. His explanation is blunt: "only Big Tech screens on credentials," and he never wanted Big Tech anyway — in his words, a place that "grinds down individual will and turns people into cogs." As for a safety net, his answer: he doesn't think about one, because "no one can catch me if I fall."

## Six arenas in three years: iteration as the method

Max didn't start a company right after dropping out. He first joined MiraclePlus — the accelerator founded by Qi Lu — where, on the research team, he "systematically learned how to start a startup": whether to hire, whether to raise, who should be your co-founder, what direction to pick. Every step, he found, could be quantified. The stint gave him an investor's-eye framework, and the judgment he'd lean on at every later fork.

The second stop was an AI education startup, where he served as COO; the company closed an angel round within three months of founding. Then commercialization taught him a brutal lesson: selling education software ran on banquets and baijiu — the team's dark joke was the "3D principle" (drink, drink, drink). As 36Kr reported, at one dinner in Changsha with an education-company boss, he forced down an entire bottle of baijiu to keep the deal alive. He closed the deal, and concluded it was meaningless: this industry competed on relationships, not product, and "it's nowhere near the stage where deals should be won at the dinner table." He wound the project down without hesitation. Buried in that story is one of his iron rules: when an industry's rules fundamentally conflict with what you believe a product should win on, cutting losses matters more than sunk costs.

After briefer stops at Sapient and Bonjour came the role that made his name: CMO of ListenHub, the AI podcast product. He grew its ARR from zero to $3 million; the overseas cold start took a $20,000 budget and returned roughly 300,000 impressions and 5,000 users on day one. Behind those numbers is classic growth-hacking craft: small budget, fast validation, content-driven distribution.

On June 4, 2026, he announced his next move on X, and the post is worth quoting: "I didn't join @AnthropicAI — I chose to join @lobehub as MAS & Head of Growth… Their team's frontier exploration of agents won me over, and LobeHub is one of the very few application teams I've seen with their own benchmark (Eval is everything). I've seen what the next generation of agents looks like, and the further future — continual learning, self-evolution, and True Agents. If you've seen the future, why not join it?" Turning down Anthropic for an open-source team is, in itself, his bet on the long-term value of open ecosystems.

Do the math on the cadence: three years, six arenas, a role change every three to six months. Traditional career wisdom calls that "unstable." In an industry inside its window period, it's a deliberate method — replace planning with practice, let each stop answer exactly one question, and move on the moment you have the answer. MiraclePlus gave him the framework, the education startup gave him the B2B lesson, ListenHub gave him a reusable growth playbook, and LobeHub gives him a ticket to the agent era.

## Community leverage: AdventureX and a hackathon every week

If the job titles are the visible thread of his story, community is the hidden one. He is one of the core organizers of AdventureX — self-described as "China's first youth hackathon," with one unusual rule: participants must be 26 or younger. It grew from 200 people in 2024 to 900 in 2025, and according to PingWest is now among the largest hackathons in China. Alongside it runs HackathonWeekly, a non-profit tech community holding weekly hackathons in Beijing and Shenzhen.

Both projects share one belief: learning by doing — use pressure to force output. And for Max personally, community has a very practical second function: it's how he accumulated a network of 3,000+ AI practitioners. In the cold starts at ListenHub and LobeHub, that network was the seed-user pool, the word-of-mouth channel, and the hiring pipeline all at once. "Community first, product second" is the single most-reused lever in everything he does.

## Content as distribution: the compounding returns of 8,500 tweets

Max's third identity is content creator: 40,000 followers and 20M+ views on Xiaohongshu; 24,000 followers and 8,500+ posts on X (@MaxForAI); long-form first published on his WeChat account "01Founder." He tracks DeepSeek, Claude, Kimi and the rest of the frontier, publishing reviews, trend analysis, and startup retrospectives.

This isn't a traffic business — it's an asset structure. Publishing in public forces continuous input (staying current is literally a Head of Growth's job); it builds professional credibility (which pays off when organizing hackathons, striking partnerships, and recruiting early users); and it ultimately turns a personal brand into the hub connecting technology, business opportunities, and community resources. For a young person with no big-company halo, it may be the cheapest lever there is.

## The playbook: five moves he keeps reusing

Compress the story into reusable moves and he's really doing five things over and over:

1. **Window analysis**: never ask "is this path stable" — ask "how long does this window stay open, and how fast is the bar rising." The dropout decision, the AI bet, the agent bet: same algorithm every time.
2. **Practice over planning**: one cycle every 3–6 months, each stop validating exactly one hypothesis; once the answer is in, move immediately — never linger for a title or for appearances.
3. **A product-first stop-loss**: the moment an industry turns out to run on relationships instead of product (the baijiu lesson), get out — a values conflict is the highest-priority stop-loss signal there is.
4. **Community first, product second**: build density of people through hackathons and groups first; at product cold-start time, that density is a free distribution network.
5. **Content as an asset**: force every day's input into public output, and let the personal brand work for you around the clock.

## A sober look: what doesn't transfer

A recommendation is not an instruction to copy. A few things need saying plainly: his window was the 2023–2026 AI boom, when capital's hunger for AI talent lowered the credential bar — that environment may not repeat. "Two Tsinghua parents" provided a financial floor and invisible support that drastically cut the cost of his trial-and-error. The high-frequency job-hopping produced a step up every single time for him — but that's survivorship bias; the same playbook in other hands may just mean never accumulating depth. He's also only 26; the long-term returns are unproven, and the education-industry episode shows his methods hit systemic resistance in relationship-driven markets.

So what's actually worth learning isn't the act of dropping out — it's how he makes decisions. Before moving, he weighs how long the window stays open and how fast the bar is rising. When a direction turns out wrong, he's willing to cut losses immediately instead of wrestling with sunk costs. And when he had no money and no resources, he built connections and influence through the cheap stuff — running events and writing in public.

## Closing

Read the two issues side by side: last time, Yuxuan Zhang — the code half: contests, open-source contributions, top-conference papers, an engineer's optimal path. This time, Max Liu is the mirror image: he doesn't write the code, but he gets the code into more hands. A healthy open-source ecosystem needs both kinds of people. The ghfind engine can score the former but not yet the latter — which is exactly why we put him in this section: what the data can't yet cover deserves to be seen all the more.

His next chapter is at LobeHub. We'll keep tracking that repository — 80k stars, 30 contributors averaging 79.5 — and somewhere in its growth curve ahead, there will be his share.
